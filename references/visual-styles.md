# Visual style instructions

Use these original prompts as a starting point for a specific spoken video. They are editorial recipes, not copies of the upstream `mg-styles-15` prompts or a claim that its examples are bundled here. The [upstream gallery](https://github.com/Vincentwei1021/mg-styles-15) is optional visual reference.

![15 visual styles in one menu](../assets/style-menu-15.svg)

## Fill in the shot before choosing a style

For each shot, specify `{spoken point}`, `{viewer must understand}`, `{real asset or evidence}`, `{aspect ratio}`, `{safe area}`, `{duration}`, and `{brand palette}`. Preserve names, numbers, quotes, and the actual sequence of events. Keep captions readable over the motion and label illustrated reconstructions. Use one dominant visual grammar for a section; a secondary style can mark a genuine shift in story.

The prompts below describe the animation layer. Combine it with the real footage and caption plan, then inspect the result at playback size. Replace decorative motion that does not clarify the spoken point.

## 15 selectable directions

1. **Flat vector — logic and comparisons.** “Explain `{spoken point}` with two to four flat colored shapes and one unmistakable visual relation. Introduce one concept at a time; use a consistent palette, crisp edges, short eased moves, and a clear end state. Keep the speaker and captions unobstructed.” Good for cause and effect, before/after, or a short framework.
2. **Line art — reasoning and paths.** “Draw a single purposeful line that traces `{viewer must understand}` in the same order as the narration. Let branches appear only when the argument branches, and resolve them at the spoken conclusion. Use restrained strokes, wide negative space, and no unexplained icons.” Good for a process, route, or decision tree.
3. **Isometric 2.5D — systems and modules.** “Build a small isometric scene from the actual components in `{spoken point}`. Add each module as it is named, then show the flow between them with visible movement. Keep labels front-facing and legible; do not imply physical scale or data that the source does not support.” Good for product architecture and workflows.
4. **Soft 3D — one hero object.** “Render one product or idea as a tactile 3D hero object with soft studio light and a restrained camera move. Reveal only the feature the narration discusses, then hold for recognition. Keep accurate proportions and branding when a real product is shown.” Good for product openings; costly for dense explanations.
5. **Hand-drawn frame animation — human feeling.** “Use imperfect hand-drawn strokes and limited frame animation to express the human action in `{spoken point}`. Animate a gesture or expression at the key phrase, pause for the line to land, and avoid drawing claims that need real evidence.” Good for personal stakes and emotional turns.
6. **Editorial collage — stories and context.** “Arrange rights-cleared photographs, documents, and paper textures into a layered editorial composition. Bring the specific source into focus at the sentence that cites it; preserve its date, name, and readable crop. Use cutout movement to show context, never to make unrelated material look like documentary proof.” Good for history, media, and sourced cases.
7. **Liquid motion — a soft transition.** “Morph a limited color field from the current section's key shape into the next section's key shape. Make the transformation match the spoken change of topic or state. Keep the transition brief and protect nearby text and faces.” Good for chapter transitions, rarely for evidence.
8. **Shape morph — change of state.** “Start from an object representing `{old state}` and transform its geometry into `{new state}` exactly as the narration describes the change. Keep a visual anchor through the morph so the audience can track what changed.” Good for evolution, tradeoffs, and before/after explanations.
9. **Bauhaus geometry — structure and rhythm.** “Use a small set of circles, bars, and grids to map the named parts of `{spoken point}`. Time each entrance to the spoken list; reserve scale and color contrast for the most important item. End on a stable composition long enough to read.” Good for chapter openers and ordered frameworks.
10. **Synthwave / VHS — explicitly retro technology.** “Use a restrained neon palette, scan texture, and perspective grid only where the story refers to a past technology era or retro culture. Let a real dated artifact carry factual claims; keep distortion away from quotes and captions.” Good for retrospectives, not generic ‘AI’ decoration.
11. **Aurora glass — calm future-facing ideas.** “Layer translucent panels over a subtle colored glow to separate two or three concepts. Animate opacity and position gently, maintain strong text contrast, and anchor every panel to a named idea. Keep the speaker visible.” Good for speculative or conceptual passages.
12. **Variety captions — conversational emphasis.** “Highlight only the one or two spoken words that carry the joke, reversal, or surprising claim. Match the text entrance to the actual syllable and clear it before the next thought. Use a consistent type hierarchy; do not cover the standard subtitle or the face.” Good for light relief in a talking-head segment.
13. **Sticker explainer — real cases and evidence.** “Place a real screenshot, quote, or object in the center, then attach a few labeled sticker-like callouts to verifiable details. Reveal callouts in narration order. Retain source attribution and distinguish the original artifact from added annotations.” Good for product or social-post walkthroughs.
14. **Pixel art — games and developer culture.** “Translate `{spoken point}` into a small, consistent pixel grid with a limited palette and stepped movement. Use authentic screenshots for factual examples, and use pixel animation only to explain the mechanic or cultural reference.” Good when the subject itself has a game or computing context.
15. **HUD / FUI — live data and interfaces.** “Show only metrics, controls, and relations supported by `{real asset or evidence}`. Animate a scan or focus change to direct attention to the value being spoken, with large legible labels and restrained motion. Never fabricate a ‘live’ reading or imply a real interface when it is an illustration.” Good for dashboards and monitoring topics.

## Selection and proof

Choose from the information job first: a real case often needs its actual screenshot before any style treatment; a process needs a visible sequence; an emotional beat may need less motion. For a new direction, show one recommended style and at most one alternative with the exact sentence and asset each would cover. Make 2–3 episode-specific frames and a 15–20 second cut with speech, captions, and proposed sound. For a local revision, retain the approved style and inspect the changed segment.
